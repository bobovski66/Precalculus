Composing measurements and models

In both examples, one measurement feeds into another calculation. We'll begin with a moving cart and its light sensor, then look at a meter that reports sound in decibels.

← Course LMS

1. A cart moving away from a light

A cart carries a light sensor directly away from a small, steady lamp. In this idealized model, its distance from the lamp after \(t\) seconds is

\[d(t)=2+0.5t\quad\text{meters},\qquad t\geq0.\]

The sensor reports a relative light reading of \(100\) units at one meter. Its reading at distance \(d>0\) follows the inverse-square model

\[L(d)=\frac{100}{d^2}.\]

The clock tells us the cart's distance, and the distance tells us the sensor's reading. To find the reading at a particular time, we put \(d(t)\) into \(L\):

\[L(d(t))=\frac{100}{(2+0.5t)^2}.\]

At \(t=4\) seconds, the distance is \(4\) meters and the reading is \(100/16=6.25\) relative units. Doubling distance divides the reading by four.

Relative light reading versus time

The curve shows the idealized model for a point-like source and unobstructed light.

Our clock begins at \(t=0\), so the meaningful times are \(t\geq0\). Although the formula could also be calculated for some negative times, those would describe a different situation.

Work with the model

  1. What does the intermediate value \(d(6)\) measure? Give its value and units.
  2. Find the relative reading after six seconds.
  3. At what time will the reading fall to \(1\) unit? Remember that distance cannot be negative.
  4. Why do we calculate \(L(d(t))\) instead of \(d(L(t))\)? What kind of input does each function expect?
Worked answers

\(d(6)=5\) meters, so \(L(d(6))=4\) relative units. A reading of \(1\) requires \(d^2=100\); the distance is positive, so \(d=10\) meters and \(2+0.5t=10\) gives \(t=16\) seconds. The sensor reading is the output of \(L\); it is not a time input for \(d\).

2. An intensity measurement displayed in decibels

Imagine a sound-level meter recording a signal as it fades. In this simplified model, the signal's intensity compared with a reference intensity is

\[R(t)=10^6e^{-0.4t},\qquad t\geq0.\]

A ratio of 1 means the two intensities are equal. The meter turns this ratio into a decibel reading using \(B(R)=10\log_{10}R\). To find what the meter shows after \(t\) seconds, we use

\[B(R(t))=10\log_{10}(10^6e^{-0.4t})=60-\frac4{\ln10}t.\]

The logarithm changes the shape of the relationship: even though the intensity ratio decreases exponentially, the decibel reading falls along a straight line. It drops by about \(1.74\) decibels each second.

Read the meter

Sound intensity level versus time

The logarithm acts on the intensity ratio, not on the time. A level of zero decibels means the intensity equals the reference.

Work with the model

  1. Calculate \(R(5)\). About how many times the reference intensity is the signal after five seconds?
  2. Find \(B(R(5))\), rounding to two decimal places.
  3. Solve for the time when the meter reads \(40\) decibels.
  4. Why can the meter take the logarithm of \(R(t)\) at every \(t\geq0\)?
Worked answers

\(R(5)=10^6e^{-2}\approx135335.28\), comparing the intensity to the reference intensity. The display reads approximately \(51.31\) decibels. Solving \(60-4t/\ln10=40\) gives \(t=5\ln10\approx11.51\) seconds. Since an exponential is positive, \(R(t)>0\) for all \(t\geq0\), so the logarithm always receives a valid input.
