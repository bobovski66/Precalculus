\chapter{Systems of Linear Equations}

Suppose a school club sells notebooks for $\$3$ each and pens for $\$1$ each.
A customer buys ten items and pays $\$22$. How many notebooks and pens did
the customer buy?

Let $x$ stand for notebooks and $y$ for pens. Counting the items gives
$x+y=10$. Counting the dollars gives $3x+y=22$. Neither equation, by
itself, tells us both numbers. The answer must satisfy \emph{both equations
at the same time}. That is the idea behind a \textbf{system of equations}.

In the first equation we can write $y=10-x$. Replacing $y$ in the second
equation gives $3x+(10-x)=22$, so $2x=12$, $x=6$, and $y=4$.
Six notebooks and four pens satisfy both conditions: there are ten items,
and they cost $18+4=22$ dollars.

We will learn two ways to understand this calculation. One is to work with
the equations, as we just did. The other is to draw their graphs. Each
equation is a line, and the answer $(6,4)$ is the point where the lines
meet. As we work with larger systems, this connection between equations
and graphs will help us recognize what our answers mean.


\section{Two Equations in Two Variables}

Think about the example above. The first line contains every combination
of notebooks and pens totaling ten items. The second contains every
combination costing $\$22$. Only combinations that lie on \emph{both} lines
solve the original problem.

More generally, an equation $ax+by=c$ describes a line in the plane
(provided $a$ and $b$ are not both zero). A solution to two equations is
an ordered pair $(x,y)$ that works in each one.

Try picturing two lines on graph paper. They might cross, run parallel
without meeting, or lie exactly on top of one another. Those pictures
explain why solving a system can produce three different kinds of answers.


\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Two lines in the plane.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \emph{Crossing} at one point: a \emph{unique solution}. The lines have
        different slopes.
  \item \emph{Parallel} and distinct: \emph{no solution}. Same slope, different
        intercepts; the lines never meet.
  \item \emph{Coincident} (the same line): \emph{infinitely many solutions}. Every
        point of the shared line solves both equations.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyidea}

\begin{keyidea}
For a system of two genuine line equations, the possibilities are one solution,
no solution, or infinitely many solutions. There is no way for two distinct
straight lines to meet in exactly two places.
\end{keyidea}

Keep these three pictures in mind as you work. The algebra will tell us
which picture belongs to a particular pair of equations.

\section{Solving by Substitution}

The notebook-and-pen example used \textbf{substitution}. We found an
expression for $y$ in one equation and used that expression in the other.
This left an equation with just $x$, which was easier to solve.

Here is the general habit. First, choose an equation in which one variable
is easy to isolate. Next, replace that variable in the other equation.
Solve the resulting equation, and then return to either original equation
to find the second value. Finally, check the pair in \emph{both} equations.
Let's work through each of the three possible outcomes.


\begin{example}[a unique solution by substitution]
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
2x+y&=7,\\
3x-2y&=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first equation is easy to rearrange: $y=7-2x$. Because this
expression equals $y$, we can replace $y$ in the second equation:
\[
3x-2(7-2x)=0.
\]
Distribute and combine like terms: $3x-14+4x=0$, so $7x=14$ and
$x=2$. Put $x=2$ back into $y=7-2x$ to get $y=3$.

We have found $(2,3)$. Check the result in both originals:
$2(2)+3=7$ and $3(2)-2(3)=0$. It works in both places, so it is
the intersection point.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[detecting no solution]
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
x-y&=1,\\
x-y&=4.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here the two left sides are identical, but the right sides disagree. The system is
asking the same expression to equal two different numbers at once.

Substituting $x=y+1$ from the first equation into the second gives $(y+1)-y=4$, that
is $1=4$, a false statement. A contradiction signals that the lines are parallel and
distinct: there is no solution.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[detecting infinitely many]
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
x+2y&=3,\\
2x+4y&=6.
\end{aligned}
\]
Notice that doubling the first equation produces the second. Although
two equations are written down, they describe the same line.

Substituting $x=3-2y$ into the second equation gives $2(3-2y)+4y=6$, that is $6=6$,
always true. Every point on $x+2y=3$ solves the system; the solution set is that
entire line. Writing $y=t$ as a free parameter, the solutions are $(3-2t,\,t)$ for
all $t$.
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}
Pay attention to what is left after you substitute. An ordinary equation
may determine a value. A false statement, such as $1=4$, tells you that no
pair can satisfy both equations. A true statement, such as $6=6$, means
the second equation has placed no additional restriction on the first.
\end{keyidea}

If you get a contradiction or an always-true statement, don't try
to force an answer for $x$ or $y$. Go back to the original lines and
explain what their relationship must be.

\section{Three Equations in Three Variables}

We have worked with two unknowns because we can draw their graphs on
ordinary graph paper. What if a problem has three unknown quantities?

An equation such as $x+y+z=6$ allows many combinations of values. In
three-dimensional space its graph is a \textbf{plane}, rather than a line.
A second equation describes another plane, and a third describes a third
plane. A solution must belong to all three planes at once.

Imagine the floor and two walls of a room. All three surfaces meet at a
corner, which gives one common point. Other arrangements may have no
common point, or may share a whole line. We still need to decide whether
the system has one solution, none, or infinitely many; only the
pictures have changed.


\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Three planes in space.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \emph{Unique solution:} the three planes meet at a single point, like three
        walls meeting at a corner.
  \item \emph{No solution:} the planes have no common point, for instance two
        parallel planes, or three forming an open triangular prism that never closes.
  \item \emph{Infinitely many:} the planes share a whole line, or all coincide in a
        single plane.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyidea}

\section{Back-Substitution and Triangular Form}

Solving three equations by substitution can get messy when every
equation contains $x$, $y$, and $z$. It helps to arrange the equations
so that the last one contains only $z$, the one above contains $y$
and $z$, and the first contains all three variables.

This arrangement is called \textbf{triangular form}. Start at the bottom,
where the last variable is easy to find. Then substitute that number into
the equation above it. Continue upward until you know all three values.
Because we work upward through the equations, the method is called
\textbf{back-substitution}.


\begin{keyidea}
A system is in \emph{triangular form} when each successive equation drops a variable,
leaving one equation in a single unknown. Solve that equation, then
\emph{back-substitute} the value upward to peel off the remaining variables in turn.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{example}[back-substitution in a triangular system]
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
x+2y-z&=3,\\
y+2z&=4,\\
3z&=9.
\end{aligned}
\]
The third equation gives $z=3$. Substitute into the second: $y+6=4$, so $y=-2$.
Substitute both into the first: $x+2(-2)-3=3$, so $x-7=3$ and $x=10$. The unique
solution is $(10,-2,3)$. Each step uses only values already found, moving upward.
\end{example}

Of course, most systems do not arrive already in triangular form.
We have to make that form ourselves. The trick is \textbf{elimination}:
add or subtract equations so that one variable disappears. Every step
should preserve the combinations that satisfy the original equations.
We are replacing difficult equations with easier ones, rather than
changing the question.

\begin{example}[reducing to triangular form, then back-substituting]
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
x+y+z&=6,\\
2x-y+z&=3,\\
x+2y-z&=2.
\end{aligned}
\]
We want to remove $x$ from the last two equations. Subtract twice
the first equation from the second:
$(2x-y+z)-2(x+y+z)=3-12$, giving $-3y-z=-9$.
Subtract the first equation from the third to get $y-2z=-4$.
Now the system reads
\[
\begin{aligned}
x+y+z&=6,\\
-3y-z&=-9,\\
y-2z&=-4.
\end{aligned}
\]
There is no $x$ in the last two equations anymore. Next remove $y$.
Multiply $y-2z=-4$ by $3$ and add it to $-3y-z=-9$.
The $y$ terms cancel, leaving $-7z=-21$, so $z=3$.

Now work back upward: $y-2(3)=-4$ gives $y=2$, and
$x+2+3=6$ gives $x=1$. The solution is $(1,2,3)$.
Check it in each original equation:
$1+2+3=6$, $2-2+3=3$, and $1+4-3=2$.

Notice the order of the work: eliminate $x$, then $y$; solve
for $z$; and finally work back to $y$ and $x$.
\end{example}

\begin{pitfall}
You may swap two equations, multiply an equation by a \emph{nonzero}
number, or add a multiple of one equation to another. These steps
can be reversed, so they keep the same solutions. Never multiply an
equation by zero: that would erase a condition the solution must satisfy.
\end{pitfall}

\section{An Application}

Systems are useful whenever several pieces of information must all be
true. A word problem often provides one condition about a total, another
about a cost or concentration, and perhaps a third about how the
quantities compare.

Before writing equations, decide what each variable will count or
measure. Then translate one condition at a time. Check that the units
make sense: adding liters gives liters, while adding amounts of an
ingredient gives an amount of that ingredient.


\begin{example}[mixing for a target]
A chemist combines three solutions of $10\%$, $20\%$, and $50\%$ concentration to
make $1$ liter of $22\%$ solution, using twice as much of the $10\%$ as the $50\%$.
Let $x,y,z$ be the liters of the $10\%$, $20\%$, $50\%$ solutions. The conditions are
\[
\begin{aligned}
x+y+z&=1 &&\text{(total volume)},\\
0.10x+0.20y+0.50z&=0.22 &&\text{(total active ingredient)},\\
x-2z&=0 &&\text{(twice as much }10\%\text{ as }50\%).
\end{aligned}
\]
From the third equation, $x=2z$. Substituting into the first two equations and solving
the resulting two-variable system gives $z=0.2$, $x=0.4$, and $y=0.4$ liters. Three
plain-language conditions become three linear equations, and their common solution is
the recipe.
\end{example}

When you finish a word problem, answer it in the language of the
question. For the mixture, that means reporting how many liters of each
solution to use, and checking that they make one liter at the requested
concentration. A solution to the algebra is useful only when it
makes sense in the original situation.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercises}
\item Describe the geometry and the number of solutions for each:
(a) two lines with different slopes; (b) two distinct lines with equal slope;
(c) one line written two equivalent ways.

\item Solve by substitution: $\begin{aligned}x+y&=5\\ 2x-y&=1\end{aligned}$ and check
both equations.

\item Solve by substitution and identify the outcome:
$\begin{aligned}3x-y&=2\\ 6x-2y&=10\end{aligned}$.

\item Solve and identify the outcome:
$\begin{aligned}x-2y&=4\\ -2x+4y&=-8\end{aligned}$, writing the solution set with a
free parameter if appropriate.

\item Explain why a linear system can never have exactly two solutions, in terms of
the geometry of lines and planes.

\item Describe two distinct geometric arrangements of three planes that each yield no
solution.

\item Solve the triangular system by back-substitution:
$\begin{aligned}x-y+2z&=5\\ y-z&=1\\ 4z&=8\end{aligned}$.

\item Reduce to triangular form and solve:
$\begin{aligned}x+y+z&=4\\ 2x+y-z&=3\\ x-2y+3z&=11\end{aligned}$, and check your
solution.

\item Solve and classify:
$\begin{aligned}x+y+z&=2\\ 2x+2y+2z&=4\\ x-y+z&=0\end{aligned}$, describing the
solution set geometrically.

\item A theater sells adult tickets at \$12 and child tickets at \$8. One evening
$200$ tickets bring in \$2000. Set up and solve a system for the numbers of each
kind.

\item A shop blends coffee at \$8, \$10, and \$14 per pound into $10$ pounds selling
at \$11 per pound, using equal amounts of the two cheaper beans. Write and solve the
system for the three weights.

\item Explain why adding a multiple of one equation to another leaves the solution
set unchanged, and why scaling an equation by zero is forbidden.
\end{exercises}
