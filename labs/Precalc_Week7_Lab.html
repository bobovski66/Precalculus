Objectives

  • Evaluate a logarithm by asking for the exponent.
  • Apply the product, quotient, and power laws of logarithms.
  • Use change of base to compute and to solve exponential equations.
  • Count information in bits using base-2 logarithms.

Problem 1 — Logarithms as exponents

Each logarithm asks: to what power must the base be raised to give the argument?
(a) Evaluate \(\log_2 8\), \(\log_{10}1000\), and \(\log_3\tfrac19\).
Show solution

\(3,\ 3,\ -2\).

\(2^{3}=8\); \(10^{3}=1000\); \(3^{-2}=\tfrac19\). Each value is the exponent that produces the argument.

(b) Evaluate \(\ln e^{4}\) and \(\log_7 1\).
Show solution

\(4\) and \(0\).

\(\ln\) and \(e^{x}\) undo each other, so \(\ln e^{4}=4\). And \(7^{0}=1\), so \(\log_7 1=0\); the logarithm of \(1\) is \(0\) for every base.

Problem 2 — The laws of logarithms

Expand \(\log_b\dfrac{x^{3}y}{z}\) into a sum and difference of logarithms \(p\log_b x+q\log_b y+r\log_b z\).
(a) Enter the coefficients \(p\), \(q\), and \(r\).
Show solution

\(3\log_b x+\log_b y-\log_b z\), so \(p=3,\ q=1,\ r=-1\).

The product becomes a sum, the quotient a difference, and the exponent \(3\) comes down as a coefficient.

(b) Explain why \(\log_b(M+N)\) cannot be expanded the way a product can, and state what \(\log_b M+\log_b N\) actually equals.
Show solution

The laws apply to products, quotients, and powers, never to sums; \(\log_b M+\log_b N=\log_b(MN)\).

A sum inside the logarithm has no expansion rule. The sum of two logarithms is the logarithm of the product, an entirely different quantity from \(\log_b(M+N)\).

Problem 3 — Change of base and solving

A calculator gives \(\ln\) and \(\log\); change of base reaches any other.
(a) Compute \(\log_2 10=\dfrac{\ln 10}{\ln 2}\) to four decimals.
Show solution

\(\log_2 10\approx3.3219.\)

\(\dfrac{\ln 10}{\ln 2}=\dfrac{2.302585}{0.693147}\approx3.3219\). It lies between \(\log_2 8=3\) and \(\log_2 16=4\), closer to \(3\).

(b) Solve \(3^{x}=20\) using logarithms, to four decimals.
Show solution

\(x=\dfrac{\ln 20}{\ln 3}\approx2.7268.\)

Take a logarithm of both sides: \(x\ln 3=\ln 20\). The logarithm extracts the exponent, which is what it was built to do.

Problem 4 — Information in bits

The information needed to identify one of \(N\) equally likely outcomes is \(\log_2 N\) bits, the number of yes/no questions to pin it down.
(a) How many bits identify one outcome from \(16\), and from \(256\)?
Show solution

\(4\) bits and \(8\) bits.

\(\log_2 16=4\) since \(2^{4}=16\); \(\log_2 256=8\) since \(2^{8}=256\).

(b) An event of probability \(p=\tfrac1{32}\) carries \(-\log_2 p\) bits when it occurs. Enter the number of bits.
Show solution

\(5\) bits.

\(-\log_2\tfrac1{32}=\log_2 32=5\). A rare event carries more information when it occurs.

(c) A system combines an \(8\)-state component with a \(4\)-state component. Verify that bits add: enter \(\log_2 8\), \(\log_2 4\), and \(\log_2 32\) for the combined \(8\times4\) system.
Show solution

\(3+2=5\): the bits add.

The combined system has \(8\times4=32\) states. The product law of logarithms, \(\log_2(8\cdot4)=\log_2 8+\log_2 4\), is exactly the additivity of information across independent sources.
