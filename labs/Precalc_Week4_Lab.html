Objectives

  • Locate zeros and vertical asymptotes from the factored form.
  • Identify a hole created by a cancelled common factor.
  • Determine end behavior by comparing numerator and denominator degrees.
  • Use long division to find a slant asymptote.

Problem 1 — Zeros, asymptotes, and a hole

Consider \(f(x)=\dfrac{(x-2)(x+1)}{(x-3)(x+1)}\).
(a) A common factor cancels, leaving a hole. At what \(x\) is the hole?
Show solution

Hole at \(x=-1\).

The factor \((x+1)\) appears in both numerator and denominator, so it cancels, removing a single point rather than creating an asymptote.

(b) After reducing to \(\dfrac{x-2}{x-3}\), enter the zero and the vertical asymptote.
Show solution

Zero at \(x=2\); vertical asymptote at \(x=3\).

After cancellation, the remaining numerator root is the zero and the remaining denominator root is the vertical asymptote.

(c) Explain why the cancelled factor gives a hole and not a vertical asymptote.
Show solution

The factor cancels, so the denominator no longer vanishes there in lowest terms.

A vertical asymptote needs a denominator root that survives reduction. When a factor appears in both numerator and denominator, it divides out, and the function equals the reduced form everywhere except that one input, which is simply missing from the domain: a hole.

Problem 2 — End behavior by degree

For each function, compare numerator and denominator degrees to find the horizontal asymptote. Enter the asymptote's \(y\)-value, or type none in the text box if there is none.
(a) \(f(x)=\dfrac{3x^{2}-1}{2x^{2}+x+5}\): enter the horizontal asymptote \(y=\).
Show solution

\(y=\tfrac32\).

Equal degrees: the asymptote is the ratio of leading coefficients, \(3/2\).

(b) \(f(x)=\dfrac{4x+1}{x^{2}-9}\): enter the horizontal asymptote \(y=\).
Show solution

\(y=0\).

The numerator degree \(1\) is below the denominator degree \(2\), so the ends approach \(y=0\).

(c) \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^{3}}{x+2}\) has no horizontal asymptote. Explain why, in terms of the degrees.
Show solution

The numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree, so the ratio grows without bound.

For large \(x\) the function behaves like \(x^{3}/x=x^{2}\), which increases without bound rather than leveling off, so there is no horizontal asymptote. The end behavior follows the polynomial quotient from long division instead.

Problem 3 — A slant asymptote by long division

Analyze \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^{2}+x-2}{x-3}\).
Explore zeros and asymptotes in the Rational Functions Lab.
(a) Long division gives \(f(x)=x+4+\dfrac{R}{x-3}\). Enter the remainder \(R\).
Show solution

\(R=10\), so \(f(x)=x+4+\dfrac{10}{x-3}\).

Dividing \(x^{2}+x-2\) by \(x-3\): quotient \(x+4\), remainder \(10\).

(b) Enter the slant asymptote as \(y=mx+b\) (give \(m\) and \(b\)), and the vertical asymptote \(x=\).
Show solution

Slant asymptote \(y=x+4\); vertical asymptote \(x=3\).

The remainder term \(\dfrac{10}{x-3}\) decays to zero at the ends, so the graph approaches the quotient line \(y=x+4\). The denominator root \(x=3\) is the vertical asymptote.

(c) Factor the numerator and enter the two zeros (smaller first).
Show solution

\(x^{2}+x-2=(x+2)(x-1)\), zeros \(-2\) and \(1\).

These are the inputs where the numerator, hence \(f\), is zero.
