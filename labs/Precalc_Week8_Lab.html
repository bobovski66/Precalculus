Objectives

  • Evaluate compositions and compare the order of their operations.
  • Decompose formulas into inner and outer functions.
  • Track domain restrictions through every stage.
  • Solve composite equations by inverse operations and substitution.
  • Use the components to explain symmetry, extreme values, and end behavior.

Big idea for Week 8

A composite function carries an input through several operations. Identifying those operations helps us track the domain, undo the layers, recognize familiar equations, and explain the graph.

Keep this question in view: What is the intermediate quantity, which operation acts next, and what restrictions must hold at that stage?

Problem 1 — Follow the output into the next function

Goal: Follow the output of one function into the input of another.
Calculation → meaningCalculate the inner function first, then apply the outer rule to that result. Reversing the order can change both the formula and its output.
What to noticeThe product f(x)g(x) multiplies two separate outputs; the composition f(g(x)) passes one output into the other function.

Let \(f(x)=x^2\) and \(g(x)=2x-1\).

(a)
Find formulas for \(f(g(x))\), \(g(f(x))\), and the product \(f(x)g(x)\). Describe the order of operations for each.
(b)
Evaluate the two compositions at \(x=2\).
(c)
Open the composition explorer. Choose “Order matters,” move the input, then swap the functions. Explain why the two displayed compositions differ.
Show solution

\(f(g(x))=(2x-1)^2\); \(g(f(x))=2x^2-1\); \(f(x)g(x)=x^2(2x-1)\). At \(x=2\) the compositions give \(9\) and \(7\). In the explorer's preset the inner function adds three and the outer function squares; swapping moves the addition after the square.

Before you move on: be able to explain, without looking at the solution, what the main calculation in this problem tells you—not only what number or formula you obtained.

Problem 2 — Identify the layers

Goal: Identify the operations inside a formula and group them into component functions.
Calculation → meaningRead a nested formula from the inside outward. A useful decomposition names the intermediate expression and the operation that acts on it next.
What to noticeMore than one decomposition can be valid. Squaring a logarithm and taking a logarithm of a square put the operations in different orders.
(a)
Write \(H(x)=\ln(1+x^2)\) as \(f(g(x))\), naming both functions.
(b)
Give a three-function decomposition of \(K(x)=e^{-(x-2)^2}\). Trace the input \(x=4\) through your functions.
(c)
Explain how \((\ln x)^2\) and \(\ln(x^2)\) use the same two operations in different orders. Give the value of each at \(x=e\).
Show solution

For \(H\), use \(g(x)=1+x^2\) and \(f(u)=\ln u\). For \(K\), use \(a(x)=x-2\), \(b(u)=-u^2\), and \(c(v)=e^v\). At \(4\) the values are \(2,-4,e^{-4}\). At \(x=e\), \((\ln e)^2=1\) and \(\ln(e^2)=2\). Other valid decompositions are possible.

Before you move on: be able to explain, without looking at the solution, what the main calculation in this problem tells you—not only what number or formula you obtained.

Problem 3 — Check every stage of the domain

Goal: Find the inputs that allow every stage of a composition to be evaluated.
Calculation → meaningFirst check the domain of the inner function. Then require its output to belong to the domain of the outer function, and combine those conditions.
What to noticeA square root accepts zero; a logarithm needs a positive input; a denominator cannot be zero. These differences determine whether an endpoint is included.

Find the real domain of each function. Show the requirements imposed by the inner and outer operations, then give the combined domain.

\[A(x)=\ln(9-x^2),\qquad B(x)=\sqrt{\ln x},\qquad C(x)=\frac1{\ln x}.\]

In the explorer, choose “Logarithm domain” and test inputs on both sides of an endpoint. Then choose “Square root of a logarithm” and test \(x=0.5\), \(x=1\), and \(x=2\). Explain why \(1\) is included in the domain of \(B\) and excluded from the domain of \(C\).

Show solution

For \(A\), require \(9-x^2>0\), giving \((-3,3)\). For \(B\), require \(x>0\) and \(\ln x\geq0\), giving \([1,\infty)\). For \(C\), require \(x>0\) and \(\ln x\ne0\), giving \((0,1)\cup(1,\infty)\). A square root accepts zero; a denominator cannot be zero. The explorer uses \(\ln(4-x^2)\) for its domain example, with endpoints \(-2\) and \(2\).

Before you move on: be able to explain, without looking at the solution, what the main calculation in this problem tells you—not only what number or formula you obtained.

Problem 4 — Reverse the operations

Goal: Solve a composite equation by undoing its operations while retaining the original restrictions.
Calculation → meaningReverse one layer at a time, then check every candidate in the original equation. Undoing a square may produce two possible inputs.
What to noticeThe principal square root of x squared is the absolute value of x. Inverse identities retain the domain of the original composition.
(a)
Solve \(\ln(x^2+1)=\ln10\). Check each answer in the original equation.
(b)
Solve \(\sqrt{\ln x}=3\), stating the original domain.
(c)
A classmate writes \(\sqrt{x^2}=x\) for every real \(x\). Test \(x=-4\), then give a formula valid for every real input.
Show solution

\(x^2+1=10\), so \(x=\pm3\); both give \(\ln10\). For the radical equation, the domain is \(x\geq1\); squaring yields \(\ln x=9\), hence \(x=e^9\). At \(x=-4\), \(\sqrt{x^2}=4\). The general formula is \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\).

Before you move on: be able to explain, without looking at the solution, what the main calculation in this problem tells you—not only what number or formula you obtained.

Problem 5 — Recognize a familiar equation inside another

Goal: Use a temporary variable to recognize a familiar equation within a composite expression.
Calculation → meaningName the repeated expression u, solve the resulting quadratic, then return to the original variable x. State the allowable values of u before selecting candidates.
What to noticeFor u = eˣ, u must be positive. For u = ln x, u can be any real number while the original input must satisfy x > 0.
(a)
Solve \(e^{2x}-7e^x+12=0\) by setting \(u=e^x\). State the possible values of \(u\), factor, and return to \(x\).
(b)
Use the same substitution to solve \(e^{2x}+2e^x-3=0\). Explain what happens to any inadmissible candidate for \(u\).
(c)
Solve \((\ln x)^2-5\ln x+6=0\) by substituting \(u=\ln x\). State the domain of \(x\).
Show solution

The first equation becomes \((u-3)(u-4)=0\), giving \(x=\ln3\) or \(\ln4\). The second becomes \((u+3)(u-1)=0\); reject \(u=-3\) because \(u>0\). From \(e^x=1\), obtain \(x=0\). For the logarithmic equation, \(x>0\), \((u-2)(u-3)=0\), and \(x=e^2\) or \(e^3\).

Problem 6 — Explain the shape before calculating many points

Goal: Explain graph features by following the behavior of the component functions.
Calculation → meaningUse the inner expression to locate symmetry and extreme values. Then use the order-preserving behavior of logarithms or exponentials to interpret the output.
What to noticeAn exponential output is always positive. Changing an input before a function acts, as in F(x+h), differs from changing its output afterward.

Use the explorer's “Logarithm of a quadratic” and “Exponential peak” presets.

(a)
For \(H(x)=\ln(1+x^2)\), explain its symmetry and find its minimum, domain, and range.
(b)
For \(K(x)=e^{-x^2}\), explain its symmetry, maximum, range, and behavior as \(|x|\) becomes large.
(c)
Predict the peak and line of symmetry of \(e^{-(x-2)^2}\). Explain your prediction through its exponent.
(d)
For \(F(x)=x^2\), expand \(F(x+h)\) and compare it with \(F(x)+h\). Which changes the input before squaring?
Show solution

\(H\) is even, has minimum \((0,0)\), domain \(\mathbb R\), and range \([0,\infty)\). \(K\) is even, has maximum \((0,1)\), range \((0,1]\), and approaches zero as \(|x|\) grows. The shifted peak is \((2,1)\), with symmetry about \(x=2\). Finally, \(F(x+h)=x^2+2xh+h^2\), whereas \(F(x)+h=x^2+h\); the former changes the input.
