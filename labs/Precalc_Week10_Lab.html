Objectives

  • Write the augmented matrix of a linear system.
  • Use row operations to reach echelon form.
  • Read the outcome (unique, none, infinitely many) from the echelon form.
  • Use a matrix to interpret a physical system and recover a quadratic from data.

Problem 1 — Follow the elimination

Last week you simplified equations to find \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\). Here the same system is written as an augmented matrix. Remember: the first three columns represent \(x,y,z\), and the numbers after the bar are the constants. \[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&1&1&4\\ 2&-1&1&8\\ 1&2&-1&-3 \end{array}\right]. \]
(a) Start by getting zeros under the first entry in the \(x\)-column. Replace row 2 with \(R_2-2R_1\) and row 3 with \(R_3-R_1\). Write the two new rows, including their constants.
Check your first two rows

\(R_2-2R_1=[\,0\ -3\ -1\mid0\,]\) and \(R_3-R_1=[\,0\ 1\ -2\mid-7\,]\).

These are new versions of two equations, but the system has exactly the same solutions as before.

(b) Remove \(y\) from one of these two rows. For example, add three times the new row 3 to the new row 2. Write the row you obtain. What equation in \(z\) does it represent?
Check your elimination

\([\,0\ -3\ -1\mid0\,]+3[\,0\ 1\ -2\mid-7\,] =[\,0\ 0\ -7\mid-21\,]\). This means \(-7z=-21\).

(c) Solve for \(z\), then use the other rows to find \(y\) and \(x\). Enter the values and check them in the original three equations.
Show solution

\((x,y,z)=(2,-1,3)\). The last row gives \(z=3\). From \(y-2z=-7\), \(y=-1\). From \(x+y+z=4\), \(x=2\).

Check: \(2-1+3=4\), \(2(2)-(-1)+3=8\), and \(2+2(-1)-3=-3\).

(d) What is the advantage of writing elimination with matrices? Also name one row operation you used and explain what it means for the equations.
Compare your reasoning

The matrix keeps coefficients lined up and makes the operations easier to organize. Subtracting twice row 1 from row 2, for example, means subtracting twice the first equation from the second. Row swaps, nonzero scaling, and adding a multiple of one row to another all preserve the solutions.

Problem 2 — What does the last row tell you?

You don't always have to finish solving a system to know how many solutions it has. Read each row as an equation and decide what the entire matrix tells you.
(a) Consider \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&3&4\\0&2&-6\end{array}\right]\). How many solutions are there?
Show explanation

One solution. The bottom row gives \(2y=-6\), so \(y=-3\), and then the first gives \(x=13\). Both variables can be determined.

(b) Now look at \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&-2&5\\0&0&-4\end{array}\right]\). How many solutions can satisfy these rows?
Show explanation

No solution. The bottom row says \(0x+0y=-4\), or \(0=-4\). That's impossible, so the entire system has no solution.

(c) Finally, consider \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&-2&3\\0&1&4&2\\0&0&0&0\end{array}\right]\). There are three unknowns. What does this arrangement tell you?
Show explanation

Infinitely many solutions. The last row says \(0=0\), so it places no restriction on the variables. There are only two leading entries for three variable columns, which leaves one free variable.

(d) Optional challenge: let \(z=t\) in part (c). Use the two remaining equations to write \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of \(t\).
Check the parameterization

From \(y+4z=2\), we get \(y=2-4t\). The first row gives \(x+y-2z=3\), so \(x=1+6t\). Thus \((x,y,z)=(1+6t,\,2-4t,\,t)\).

Problem 3 — Currents in a circuit

Current is the flow of electric charge. At a junction, the current flowing in must equal the current flowing out. A circuit has three unknown branch currents \(I_1,I_2,I_3\), measured in amperes. The circuit conditions give the system \[ \begin{aligned} I_1-I_2-I_3&=0,\\ I_1+2I_2&=10,\\ 2I_2+3I_3&=9. \end{aligned} \] We will use the equations as given; you don't need any previous knowledge of electrical engineering.
(a) Write the augmented matrix with columns in the order \(I_1,I_2,I_3\). Remember the zero coefficient of \(I_3\) in the second equation and of \(I_1\) in the third.
Check the matrix

\(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&-1&-1&0\\1&2&0&10\\0&2&3&9\end{array}\right]\).

(b) Replace row 2 with \(R_2-R_1\). What is the new row? Then eliminate \(I_2\) using the last two rows to find a row involving only \(I_3\).
Check the elimination

\(R_2-R_1=[\,0\ 3\ 1\mid10\,]\). The third row remains \([\,0\ 2\ 3\mid9\,]\). Taking three times the third row minus twice the new second row gives \([\,0\ 0\ 7\mid7\,]\), or \(7I_3=7\).

(c) Solve for the three currents and enter the values in amperes.
Show solution

\(I_1=4,\ I_2=3,\ I_3=1\) amperes. The last row gives \(I_3=1\). Next \(3I_2+1=10\) gives \(I_2=3\), and \(I_1-I_2-I_3=0\) gives \(I_1=4\).

(d) Check \(I_1=I_2+I_3\) using your currents. What does this equation say about the electrical charge moving through the junction?
Compare your explanation

\(4=3+1\). Four amperes flow into the junction, and a total of four flow out. The current is balanced, consistent with conservation of electric charge.

Problem 4 — Recover a quadratic from three points

You know how to graph \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) when you are given the coefficients \(a,b,c\). Here we turn that around: we know three points on a parabola and need to find the coefficients. The graph passes through \((0,2)\), \((1,5)\), and \((3,23)\).
(a) Substitute each point into \(y=ax^2+bx+c\). What three equations do the points give you? Remember: the unknowns now are \(a,b,c\), not \(x,y\).
Check the equations

The points give \(c=2\), \(a+b+c=5\), and \(9a+3b+c=23\).

(b) Write the augmented matrix for the three equations, with columns \(a,b,c\). Then simplify using the first equation \(c=2\).
Check your setup

The matrix is \(\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}0&0&1&2\\1&1&1&5\\9&3&1&23\end{array}\right]\). The first row says \(c=2\), which leaves \(a+b=3\) and \(9a+3b=21\), or \(3a+b=7\).

(c) Solve for \(a,b,c\). Enter the coefficients of the quadratic.
Show solution

\(a=2,b=1,c=2\), so the quadratic is \(y=2x^2+x+2\). Solve \(a+b=3\) and \(3a+b=7\) to find \(a=2\) and \(b=1\).

(d) Write the quadratic equation and check that it gives the right \(y\)-value at all three \(x\)-values.
Check the model

\(y=2x^2+x+2\). At \(x=0\) it gives \(2\); at \(x=1\) it gives \(2+1+2=5\); at \(x=3\) it gives \(18+3+2=23\).
